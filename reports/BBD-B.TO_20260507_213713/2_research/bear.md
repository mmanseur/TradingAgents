## 6. THE LIQUIDITY CRISIS IS REAL—NOT A METRIC PROBLEM

### Don't Fall for the "Inventory Argument"

The bull argues: "Inventory is converting to cash, so the quick ratio is misleading."

This is backwards thinking. Here's why:

**The quick ratio exists precisely because inventory is the *least* liquid asset.** A quick ratio of 0.52 means:

- For every **$1 of current liabilities**, Bombardier has only **$0.52 in liquid assets** (cash + receivables)
- They need to **sell inventory just to stay solvent**
- If they can't sell inventory (demand shock), they're in trouble

**Current liabilities: $6.34 billion**  
**Cash + receivables: ~$2.07 billion**  
**Shortfall: $4.27 billion that must come from inventory conversion**

That's not a comfortable position. That's **debt-trap territory.**

### The Deferred Revenue Trap

Here's what the bull completely ignores: **$5.97 billion in deferred revenue is a LIABILITY, not an asset.**

When a customer pays upfront for an aircraft (common in the industry), that payment sits as **deferred revenue until delivery.** From an accounting standpoint, Bombardier must:
1. Produce and deliver the aircraft
2. Absorb all costs of production
3. Then recognize the revenue

**This means $5.97B in future revenue comes with $5.97B in future costs.** It's not free money. In fact, if costs exceed the contracted price (which happens in aerospace), these "assets" become liabilities.

The bull treats deferred revenue as if it's pure profit. **It's not—it's future operational risk.**
